#+TITLE: No Marker
#+SUBTITLE: Scene 003 — drafted 2026-09-06

* Card

- *Question:* What does Haesius see when he looks at Leina?
- *Answer:* His sister, coming back. Which is why he lets her do whatever she
  likes — it was never apathy or weakness. It is forty years of conditioning that
  says get out of the way. And it is the same reflex that will make him put her
  out of the house.
- *Period:* Leina's childhood.
- *Tone:* Dark, warm on the surface.
- *Setting:* Haesius Moore's kitchen, Blackmoor. Rain. Leina is eleven, four
  months into living there.
- *Characters:* Haesius Moore, Leina Grey. (Nume, in account.)
- *Establishes:* Haesius is not a pushover by temperament — he is trained. Also
  the first time anyone has told Leina anything about Nume as a child.

* Scene

He had gotten used to most of it. The locked study door. The candles burning past
any sane hour and the cost of them, which she paid for herself and made a point of
putting on the table in coin so he could count it. The way she said /Lemeus/ and
/Cudner/ and /Yitna/ like a man at a ledger.

What he had not gotten used to was the reading.

She sat with the book flat and one finger laid along the line she was on, and when
she got up — for water, for the privy, because someone knocked — she closed it. No
ribbon. No slip of paper. Nothing marking anything. And when she came back she
opened it and went straight on.

He watched her do it twice that evening before he said anything, and when he did
his voice came out wrong.

"You don't keep a marker."

Leina looked up. She had a way of looking up that made you feel you had opened a
door on someone's private business.

"I know the page."

"You know the page."

"Four hundred and eleven." She turned the book so he could see the number, then
turned it back, and he understood that she had done that to end the conversation
efficiently and not to prove anything, which was somehow worse.

Haesius sat down. He had come in for a hot drink and had forgotten about it
entirely.

"Your mother did that."

Leina's finger stopped moving on the page.

She did not look up this time, which he had learned meant she was paying attention
properly.

"When we were small," he said. "I moved her marker once. Out of the book — she'd
left it on the sill and I put it back in the wrong place, thinking I was being
useful. She was maybe nine. Ten." He rubbed his mouth. "She said she didn't use
one. I said everyone uses one. She said /I don't/, and I said /prove it/, and
she—"

He stopped.

"She what."

"She recited it," he said. "The page. Not the number. The page. Every word on it,
top to bottom, standing in the kitchen in her nightdress. Took her a good while.
Our mother came in halfway through and told her to be quiet and she wouldn't stop,
because I'd said prove it, and she wasn't finished proving it."

The rain went on outside.

"How long did she keep going," Leina said.

"Until she was finished."

"And then?"

"And then she looked at me," said Haesius, "and she said, /now put it back where
you found it./"

Leina made a small sound. It was not quite a laugh. She caught it before it was
anything, the way she caught everything, but he heard it, and it went through him
like cold water, because it was the exact sound Nume used to make.

She had questions. He could see them stacked up behind her face like carts at a
gate. /What else. What was she like. Was she always. Did she ever./

She said, "Thank you for the account."

/The account./ Eleven years old.

"Aye," he said.

She went back to page four hundred and eleven.

Haesius stood in his own kitchen a while longer. He had told himself, four months
ago, that he was letting the girl do as she liked because he was tired, and
because he'd never had children, and because it was easier than the alternative.

That wasn't it, and he'd known it wasn't from the first week.

He was nine years old the last time he stood in front of his sister and said /no/
to her. He could still tell you what the floor looked like. And whatever had been
in her then was in this child now, in the kitchen, in the candlelight, reading a
book with no marker in it.

You do not stand in front of that. You get out of its way and you keep the house
warm and you say nothing, and if you are very lucky it does not notice you were
ever there.

He went to bed without his drink.
