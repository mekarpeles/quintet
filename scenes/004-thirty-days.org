#+TITLE: Thirty Days
#+SUBTITLE: Scene 004 — drafted 2026-09-10, rewritten same day

* Card

- *Question:* On what terms is Ardovus Retik alive?
- *Answer:* Thirty days at a time, renewable, subject to performance. He is not
  being kept alive out of mercy — he is a line item, and the last three months
  have been slow. Which is why the Manual matters to him more than to anyone else
  in the story: it is the only thing that would ever take him off the lease.
- *Period:* Two years before Blackmoor.
- *Tone:* Dark.
- *Setting:* A Red maintenance station in a converted customs house, somewhere
  north.
- *Characters:* Ardovus Retik, a Red clerk, a captive Soul Speaker.
- *Establishes:* how the Reds' medicine works and what it costs; that the Reds'
  practitioners are equipment, not staff; that the interval is shortening; that
  Ardovus cannot conceive of a future longer than a month.

* Note on the first draft

First version was rejected — mek: "I have no idea what is going on in this
scene." Correctly. It withheld every fact (that his heart is failing, what the
station is, who the woman is, what the queue is for) and substituted atmosphere.
Underwritten, not subtle. The rewrite states the arrangement plainly and adds the
missing stake: *maintenance is an investment decision, and he has to justify the
expense every thirty days.*

* Scene

Ardovus Retik's heart had been failing since he was twenty-six. Slowly, the way a
roof fails. A physician in Tura had given him four years and had been wrong, and
the reason he had been wrong was sitting in a room at the end of this hall with
her hands in her lap.

The Reds could hold it off. Not mend it — hold it. A Soul Speaker with real skill
could go into a man and slow the thing down, and if you came back before the work
wore through, you went on living.

The work lasted about a month.

So this was the arrangement, and it had been the arrangement for eleven years: he
did what they told him, and once a month they let him keep his heart beating.
Nobody had ever written it down. Nobody needed to.

The hall had been a customs house and still smelled of tar and old fish.
Twenty-odd people on the benches, all of them somebody's investment — a
quartermaster who could remember any figure he had ever seen, a woman who had been
a champion of something, a boy of nineteen still in the part where it feels like
good fortune.

They did not talk. There is nothing to say to someone in the same position that
does not sound like a threat.

The clerk had the book open before Ardovus reached the table.

"Retik. Thirty days. Account."

This was the dangerous part. Not the room at the end of the hall — this. A man
with a pen, deciding whether you had been worth the expense.

"Stromkipre," Ardovus said. "Done. The Antry business, done, and the ledgers came
back with it. Eleven days on the Coldhurst matter and nothing to show."

"Nothing."

"The man was dead before I got there. Fever, they said." He did not shrug.
Shrugging invited discussion. "I can't collect from a grave."

The clerk wrote for a while.

Then: "Sixty-one days on the last three. That's slow."

"It is."

"Don't be slow."

"No," said Ardovus.

The woman in the room was past fifty and wore no insignia, which was how you knew.
The staff had insignia. The ones who could actually do it did not, because they
were not staff, they were equipment, and you do not pin a badge on a lathe.

"Coat and shirt."

He lay on the table and she put her hands flat on his chest, and a door opened in
him that he had not known was a door, and somebody came in and started moving
furniture. Eleven minutes. It was always about eleven.

When she lifted her hands he sat up and reached for his shirt, and she stayed
where she was with her palms braced on the table edge, shaking.

He knew exactly what that was. He had twenty years of knowing it from the inside.
It had never once occurred to him that he would recognise it on somebody else.

She said, without looking up, "Twenty-eight days. Not thirty-one."

In six years she had never said a word to him that was not procedure.

"It's shortening," she said.

"By how much."

"Come at twenty-eight."

He finished dressing. At the door he said "thank you," and she said "don't," and
that was all of it.

The clerk wrote the next date and turned the book round so he could see it, the
way they always did.

Twenty-eight days.

He had not thought about a year from now since he was twenty-six. Not evasively —
genuinely. Stop him in the street and ask what he wanted in a year and he could
not have found an answer, because the question had been taken off him so long ago
that he had lost the shape of the hole where it used to sit.

He went out into the rain and turned south, because there was a woman in a place
called Blackmoor he had been told to go and look at, and a book she was said to
own, and he had been given to understand that if he came back with it they might
do more than hold the roof up.

Twenty-eight days was plenty.
