* Scene

The rain had gone through her coat somewhere past the mill and she had walked the
last two miles wet. Her fingers had stopped hurting, which she knew was worse than
hurting. She did not run the last stretch, because running meant you had misjudged
your timing, and she had not misjudged anything. She had simply been wrong about
the weather, which was different, and the difference mattered to her.

Smithens looked up when the door went, and looked back down, and said, "Second
shelf, the Turan grammars came in."

She stood in the doorway dripping.

He did not say /you poor thing./ He did not say /does your mother know./ He got up,
with the noise old men make getting up, and dragged the reading chair six inches
nearer the fire, and sat back down at his desk without once looking at her.

She took the chair.

After a while there was tea beside her, and she had not seen him put it there.

"Mr. Smithens."

"Mm."

"I don't have the ha'penny for the grammars."

"Nobody's asked you for a ha'penny."

"Someone will."

"Then I'll tell them you had it off me and they can take it up with a dying man."
He licked his thumb and turned a page. "Drink that before it's cold, it's a waste
otherwise."

She drank it. It was too strong and there was too much of it and she drank all of
it.

Twenty minutes later he said, not turning around, "Can I ask you something, and
will you tell me the true answer and not the polite one."

"I don't know the polite one."

"No," he agreed. "That's why I'm asking."

He brought the Smithendex over — four sheets, edge to edge, black with arrows.
He'd been at it eleven years. He put it down on the arm of the chair like it was
somebody's baby.

"It's gone wrong," he said. "I can't say how. Every time a new thing comes in I
have to draw another line and now I can't read my own hand. My wife would have
said I'd made a mess and gone to bed. She'd have been right and it would not have
helped me."

Leina looked at it a long time.

"You've made the topics the fixed thing," she said. "They're not the fixed thing.
The connections are."

"Say more."

"If you wrote each one on its own scrap, you could move them. Punch a hole either
side — one for what it needs, one for what needs it. Then when a new book comes
you tie it in and nothing else has to change."

Smithens didn't say anything.

Then he got up, took a pen, and wrote it down.

That was the part. He wrote down a thing she had said. Not to test her on it
later, not to correct it, not to prove she'd said it. He wrote it down because he
thought it was worth having.

Something happened in her chest that she had no name for, and she sat very still
until it went away, the way you'd wait out a cramp.

She stayed until dark and cut forty scraps with his paper knife and did not ask
for anything.

She walked home in the rain doing the arithmetic.

He had spent: one chair moved, one cup of tea, four minutes of attention, and a
ha'penny he was never going to collect. He had received: eleven years of work
repaired by the only person in Westhaven who could have repaired it, for free, and
she would come back tomorrow and do more.

And he hadn't done it on purpose. That was the part that took her the whole walk.
He wasn't running anything. He had simply been kind, and it had worked better than
anything her mother had ever done to her.

She was cold and her feet had blistered and she was thinking about a cup of tea
like it was a formula.

She wrote it down when she got in.

She brushed Melinda's hair for the first time that Thursday.
