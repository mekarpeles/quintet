* Soap
- *Q:* What does it actually cost Leina to be nobody on purpose?
- *A:* Her arrangements are only as good as her standing, and she has none. She
  had it planned and paid for, and the plan failed publicly, and the only thing
  that would have fixed it was leverage she refuses to spend.
- Mapping demonstration, the green at Blackmoor. Leina is eleven.

She had it arranged. That was the thing. She had gone to Dessa Krell eight days
before and paid two coppers up front and two on completion, and Dessa had said
yes, and Dessa's word had been good every other time.

Then Wenn Abigale's team came one short, and Wenn asked Dessa, and Dessa went.

Went, in front of everyone, with the coppers in her pocket.

"You've had money off me," Leina said. Not loud. Just so it was on the record.

"Then come and take it back." Wenn wasn't even being cruel about it; he was
laughing, which was worse, because it meant this cost him nothing. "Go on. Or —
here's a thought — maybe if your mother bought you a bar of soap instead of
another book of foreign maps nobody can read, you'd have someone other than
orphans wanting you."

Eleven children laughed.

Leina stood on the wet grass and did the sum, because the sum was all she had.
Answering Wenn was worth nothing. Naming Dessa a thief was worth nothing and cost
her Dessa. Saying the true thing — /your mother is in Johan Lemeus's cottage on
Thursdays and I have known for a year/ — was worth a great deal, and she would
never be able to spend it twice, and this was not the day.

She got two orphans off the Tannery road, and Perrin, whose hip was bad, and
Melinda, who would have come anyway.

They came third. They almost always came third.

And the witnessing adult signed her folio /adequate/, which was the entire point,
and she walked home with her ledger balanced and her eyes hot and did not cry,
because crying was not on the list of things that changed outcomes.

* The Board
- *Q:* What does the town tell Leina she is?
- *A:* Idle, unsponsored, unteachable, and her mother's fault. Every word of it
  is the cover working, and she cannot correct one syllable.
- The placement hall, Westhaven. Leina is twelve.

Haesius presented the folio because a guardian has to. His hands shook the whole
way through and he apologised twice to men who had not asked him for anything.

There were three of them at the table. The one in the middle did the talking.

"Adequate throughout," he said. "Adequate. Adequate. Adequate. Do you know how
many of these I read in a season?"

"No, sir."

"Nor do I, and that's rather the point." He turned a page. "No fee lodged. No
sponsor's word. Nobody has written a line for you, girl — nobody at all, and in a
town this size that says something all by itself."

Smithens would have. Yitna would have. Cudner would have put her name to it and
meant it.

"Your mother didn't come."

"No, sir."

"She didn't come to the last one either. I'll be frank with you, because someone
should: your mother is not well and everyone knows it, and she is also idle, and
between the two of them she has done you no favours. That house is a byword.
You've no money, no connections, no application, and you can't keep your attention
on anything long enough to be worth teaching." He shut the folio. "And she's
plainly given up on you, which makes you a bad influence besides."

/Idle./ Leina looked at the tabletop and counted to four.

She thought about her mother at half past five in the morning, in the dark, in the
cold, with a candle, for twelve years.

"Scribe," said the man. "With your uncle, since he'll have you. Nobody else
would."

"Thank you, sir."

Outside, the list went up on the board, and Wenn Abigale had Scientist, which he
would be perfectly fine at, and his mother was there, and she cried, and Leina
stood in the rain and watched a woman be proud of her son and did not spend the
thing she was holding.

She never did spend it.

* The Brush
- *Q:* What does Leina want?
- *A:* Unanswered on the page, and that is the point. The whole want is in the
  refusal, and the reader carries it for her.
- Haesius's back room. Leina is eleven.

Melinda sat on the floor between Leina's knees while Leina worked the tangles out
of the back of her head, which took a while, because nobody at the Sauveneux house
had done it in a month.

"You're good at this."

"It's not difficult."

"It is, though. Ma pulls." Melinda tipped her head back and looked up, upside down
and cheerful. "Do you want me to do yours after?"

Leina's hands stopped.

It was not a long pause. Half a second, maybe less; Melinda did not notice it, and
Melinda noticed very little.

"No," Leina said.

"I wouldn't pull."

"No. Sit forward, I've not finished."

"All right." And Melinda sat forward and started telling her something about a dog
on the Westhaven road, and was completely untroubled, and was still talking about
it four minutes later.

Leina brushed her hair and listened and made the right noises in the right places,
and thought about nothing at all, very deliberately, for the rest of the evening.

* The Visitor
- *Q:* Is there anybody Nume answers to?
- *A:* Odile. And Leina sees it once, at eight, and spends nine years unable to
  explain what she saw.
- The Grey house. Leina is eight.

There was a woman at the table when Leina came down, and her mother was pouring.

Her mother never poured. People poured for her mother.

The woman was old but not the kind of old that sits down. She had wet boots by the
fire and had clearly walked, and nobody walked to Blackmoor.

"—and I'd have done it the other way," the woman was saying.

"I know you would."

"Do you know why?"

And Nume said, "Yes," and then, after a moment, "No. Tell me."

Leina stood on the third stair and did not move a muscle, because her mother had
just been /wrong out loud/ and had said so, and had asked to be told, and was now
sitting with her hands in her lap like a girl at a bench.

She looked small. That was the thing Leina could not get past. Her mother, who
filled every room she had ever been in, was small at her own table.

The woman explained something Leina was too far away to hear, and Nume listened
all the way to the end without interrupting once, which Leina had not known she
could do.

Then the woman turned round on the bench without being told anyone was there and
said, "You'll be Madeleina."

"Yes."

"Come here then."

Leina looked at her mother. Her mother nodded.

The woman took her chin, turned her face one way and then the other, and let go.
She did not smile and she did not say anything kind, and Leina liked her
enormously and immediately and could not have told you why.

"Well," said the woman. "You've got the whole of it, haven't you."

"The whole of what?"

"Ask your mother in about ten years." She picked up her cup. "And when she tells
you, remember that I asked first."

She was gone before Leina woke up. Nobody explained. Nobody ever mentioned it.

Leina thought about it for the next nine years.

* The Margin
- *Q:* When does Leina learn she has a name?
- *A:* In a river town, eleven months out, from a stranger — and it has grown on
  its own, out of a lie Yitna told to end a conversation, and she cannot tell
  whether it is an asset or a debt.
- A tavern room in a river town. Leina is thirteen.

The woman at the next table had a bad shoulder and had been talking about it for
twenty minutes, which was how Leina knew she was about to hear something.

"—and my sister took it to the margin," the woman said. "Two seasons back."

The man grunted.

"Don't make that face. She's walking."

"Anyone can walk."

"She /wasn't/ walking, Denn. That's the whole of my point. And they never ask you
for anything, that's the part I can't make sense of — it comes back to you through
three people and there's no coin in it anywhere."

Ardovus, four feet away, turned a page of the thing he was pretending to read.

Leina kept her spoon moving.

It had taken her a moment to work out what she was hearing. Yitna's word, three
hundred miles from Yitna, in a river town Leina had never been to, out of the
mouth of a stranger — a word that had started life as /I'm not telling you where I
had it/ and had, somewhere in the last year, quietly stopped being an evasion and
started being a person.

An old man, the woman seemed to think. Somewhere north. Possibly several of them.

"Sounds like a story," said the man.

"Aye, well. My sister's walking."

Leina finished her soup at exactly the rate she had been finishing it before.

Across the table, Ardovus said, without looking up, "You've gone very still."

"I'm tired."

"Mm," he said, and turned another page, and did not believe her, and let it go —
and she understood that he had let it go on purpose, and that he would be filing
it, and that one day it would be worth something to him.

She lay awake most of that night.

Not out of fear. She kept turning it over the way you turn a coin you've found in
the road: /they never ask you for anything. There's no coin in it anywhere./

Somebody had built a thing that ran on nothing, out of a lie Dr. Yitna told to end
a conversation.

And it was hers, and it had grown while she wasn't looking, and she had not paid
for a single bit of it.

She could not work out, lying there, whether that was an asset or a debt.
