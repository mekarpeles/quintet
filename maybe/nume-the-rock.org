* Card

- *Question:* Why does Nume teach her daughter that being right is dangerous?
- *Answer:* Because the alternative was explaining, and explaining meant a
  ten-year-old carrying the most dangerous fact in the world around a hamlet where
  she runs errands for four adults and cannot yet lie to anyone who matters. Nume
  costed it, chose to make it hurt, and knew the price to the decimal.
- *Period:* Leina's childhood. Leina is ten. The evening of the Oracle rock.
- *Tone:* Dark.
- *POV:* Nume — the only scene so far written from inside her.
- *Setting:* The Grey kitchen, Blackmoor.
- *Uses:* the four-second touch (scene 002), the casebook, the notices.

* Scene

The girl came in from the rock with her boots on and said, "Somebody died today.
Three towns over, I think. North."

Nume was cutting bread and did not stop cutting bread.

"What makes you say that."

"I felt it." Leina got her coat off. She was pleased. That was the thing Nume
would think about later, at two in the morning and at intervals for the rest of
her life — that the child was /pleased/, the way you are pleased when you find you
can whistle.

"Felt it how."

"Like when the cart goes past the house and the cups move. Except in my chest, and
it wasn't the cart." She considered. "It was a man."

/A man./ Not a person. A man.

Nume set the knife down flat, which she did so that her hands would have somewhere
to be.

Four questions came up at once and she chose the smallest.

"What time."

"After the bell. Not long after."

Two hours ago, then. Coldhurst was three towns north and eleven days by road and
there would be a notice in eight or nine days if it was anybody, and it would say
fever, and it would be a man, and Nume already knew — with a certainty that had
nothing to do with evidence and everything to do with thirty years of this —
exactly which man.

And her daughter had felt it happen from a rock outside a hamlet, at ten years
old, without being taught, without trying, and had come home and taken her coat
off and said so /at the door/.

To her. This time. To her, in a locked house, with no one in it.

Next time it would be to Melinda. Or to Lemeus. Or to the woman at the market who
asks children questions because she likes the sound of them. Or, in eleven years,
in front of somebody with a ledger.

There was no version of the next hour in which Nume got to be glad.

She could explain. She costed it while she stood there with her hand on the knife.
Explaining meant the word /Oracle/ in this house, in this child's mouth, with all
the follow-up questions attached, and then a ten-year-old carrying the single most
dangerous fact in the world around a hamlet where she did errands for four adults
and could not yet lie to anyone who mattered. Eighteen months, Nume thought. Two
years at the outside.

The alternative was to make it hurt.

She knew exactly what it would cost. That was the part nobody would ever credit
her with — she did not do it blind, or in temper, or because she was unwell. She
stood in her kitchen with a loaf half cut and priced it out, and the price was her
daughter, a little, today, and more later, and she paid it.

"Sit down," Nume said.

Leina sat.

"Say it again."

"I felt a man die—"

"You felt a man die. Three towns off. Through the ground, was it? Through the
air?" Nume let that sit. "Which. You'll have a mechanism."

"I don't—"

"You don't. So what you have is a feeling, and a story you put on top of it, and
you walked in my door and said it as a fact." She kept her voice absolutely level,
which was worse than shouting and both of them knew it. "What's the word for
that?"

Leina's ears had gone red. "A hypothesis."

"No. A hypothesis is offered. You /announced/. There's a word for people who
announce feelings as facts and it isn't a kind one, and if you say a thing like
that outside this house, that is what people will decide you are, and they will be
right." She picked the knife back up. "Say it."

"...I don't have a mechanism."

"Again."

"I don't have a mechanism."

"Then you don't have anything. Wash your hands."

And Nume watched it land. Watched the pleasure go out of the child's face and
something careful come in behind it, like a shutter drawn from the inside, and she
thought: /good./

And also: /that was the last time she will ever bring me something for nothing./

Both of those were true at the same time and she had known they would be before
she opened her mouth.

Nine days later the Weekly carried a notice on page four. Fever. A man.
Forty-four.

Nume read it at the table with her daughter three feet away doing figures, and did
not make a sound, and went and put two fingers on the spine of the book on the low
shelf for four seconds, and came back.

That night she got the chest out and opened the casebook and wrote:

/GREY, Madeleina. Of Blackmoor. Aged ten./

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she thought about the eleven other things she should have said in that
kitchen, all of which were true, all of which the child would need, several of
which she would not survive without — and she found she could not make herself do
it, not tonight, not to a ten-year-old washing her hands at the basin, and she
despised herself with a completeness that would have frightened anyone who saw it.

She left the outcome column blank.
